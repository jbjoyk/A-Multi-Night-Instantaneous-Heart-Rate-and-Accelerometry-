## Structured Summary

### Background
Consumer smartwatches estimate sleep stages from ~0.2 Hz instantaneous heart rate (IHR) and accelerometry, yet translation is hindered by inter-individual domain shift, benchmark leakage, unquantified calibration thresholds, and unknown generalisation to in-lab polysomnography (PSG).

### Methods
Under subject-disjoint 5-fold cross-validation in BIDSleep (N=47 healthy adults, 246 nights; Apple Watch and Dreem 2 EEG), we benchmarked frozen Model A (163,940 parameters; 33.34-fold compression vs official 5,465,768-parameter SLAMSS-IFS) and dynamic INT8 quantisation. Across N=46 eligible participants (152 evaluation nights), we tested 2-night supervised fine-tuning against a pre-registered +1.50 percentage-point (pp) threshold, donor-night, from-scratch, and quality-gated unsupervised controls, 1-to-4-night scaling (N=33 common cohort, 64 nights), and exploratory stage-coverage gating. Zero-shot external validation used single-night in-lab PSG from PhysioNet SleepAccel (N=31; n=25 quality-clean primary).

### Findings
On BIDSleep (N=47), Model A achieved 64.79% Macro-F1 (66.18% accuracy) vs 67.37% (68.75%) for SLAMSS-IFS (-2.58 pp, p=1.98×10^-6); dynamic INT8 (309.54 KiB, 47.7 ms/night) preserved Macro-F1 (+0.0011 pp, p=0.7892). Confirmatory 2-night fine-tuning (N=46) yielded +1.01 pp (95% CI -0.17 to +1.99, p=0.00131), inconclusive against the +1.50 pp threshold. Monotonic 1-to-4-night scaling (N=33: +1.75 to +2.71 pp, Page's L p=0.01187) and exploratory stage-coverage gating (+1.33 pp, 95% CI +0.49 to +2.12, cutting worst collapse 61% to -6.40 pp) supported k≥3 calibration. On zero-shot external single-night PSG (n=25 primary single-model evaluation), SLAMSS-IFS's lead attenuated 78% to -0.57 pp (60.51% vs 61.08%, p=0.5782; N=31 -1.62 pp, p=0.3468), while Dreem 2-to-PSG label-prior shift drove Deep (+10.65%) and REM (+10.13%) overestimation.

### Interpretation
On zero-shot external single-night PSG, lightweight smartwatch models showed no statistically significant difference in 4-class Macro-F1 versus 33-fold larger server architectures and reliably tracked total sleep time, whereas stage proportions required label-prior calibration. Within-person fine-tuning was inconclusive at two nights, requiring at least three calibration nights and exploratory stage-coverage gating.

## Research in context

### Evidence before this study
We searched PubMed, IEEE Xplore, arXiv, medRxiv, and public sleep repositories (PhysioNet, the National Sleep Research Resource [NSRR], and OpenNeuro) from Jan 1, 2018, to Nov 15, 2026, without language restrictions, using the search terms `("sleep staging" OR "sleep stage") AND ("photoplethysmography" OR "instantaneous heart rate" OR "smartwatch" OR "wearable") AND ("personalization" OR "fine-tuning" OR "multi-night" OR "adaptation")`. We included peer-reviewed studies, preprints, and open datasets evaluating multi-class sleep stage classification from wrist-worn optical photoplethysmography (PPG) or derived instantaneous heart rate (IHR) and accelerometry against reference electroencephalography (EEG) or laboratory polysomnography (PSG). Consumer-grade PPG and accelerometry enable longitudinal home sleep monitoring, yet mapping peripheral autonomic signals to central EEG sleep stages remains vulnerable to inter-individual physiological variance and data leakage across epochs or nights [1–5]. Walch and colleagues [2] demonstrated 3-class sleep staging from Apple Watch IHR and motion in 31 adults on single-night PSG, and Song and colleagues [1] introduced the 5.47-million-parameter SLAMSS-IFS sequence architecture on the multi-night BIDSleep dataset using a single 11-subject holdout. Crucially, van der Aar and colleagues [3] demonstrated in 59 adults with suspected sleep disorders that supervised fine-tuning on high-rate raw PPG improved staging in 82.5% of individuals once at least three calibration nights ($k \ge 3$) were available. However, our systematic repository audit confirmed that no second public multi-night healthy-adult smartwatch and EEG dataset currently exists, leaving leakage-free calibration thresholds on low-rate (~0.2 Hz) smartwatch IHR, negative-transfer safeguards, matched-protocol edge compression trade-offs, and zero-shot generalisation to gold-standard in-lab PSG unquantified.

### Added value of this study
Under pre-registered, strictly chronological, subject-disjoint evaluation on BIDSleep ($N=47$) [1] and strictly zero-shot external validation on PhysioNet SleepAccel single-night in-lab PSG ($N=31$; $n=25$ quality-clean primary) [2], this study delivers four translational advances. First, we show that while pre-registered confirmatory two-night fine-tuning ($+1.01\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $-0.17\text{ to }+1.99$) was inconclusive against its locked $+1.50\text{ pp}$ superiority threshold, within-person adaptation on ~0.2 Hz Apple HealthKit IHR scales monotonically across $k=1\text{–}4$ calibration nights ($p=0.01187$ by Page's $L$ trend test [9]), converging with clinical raw-PPG findings [3] once $k \ge 3$ nights are available, whereas exploratory pre-adaptation stage-coverage gating prevents extreme-tail negative transfer (reducing worst-case participant collapse by 61% from $-16.34\text{ pp}$ to $-6.40\text{ pp}$). Second, we quantify the exact matched 5-fold cross-validation trade-off between a 163,940-parameter dynamic 8-bit integer (INT8) model (309.54 KiB in-memory weights, 47.7 ms per night on central processing unit [CPU]) and the 33.34-fold larger official SLAMSS-IFS server architecture ($-2.58\text{ pp}$ Macro-F1). Third, we evaluate quality-gated unsupervised information-maximisation (InfoMax) adaptation on calibration nights without EEG labels, demonstrating a modest, statistically significant sub-benchmark gain ($+0.21\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $+0.04\text{ to }+0.39$) that cannot substitute for supervised calibration. Fourth, we demonstrate on strictly zero-shot external single-night PSG that SLAMSS-IFS's BIDSleep Macro-F1 advantage attenuates by 78% to show no statistically significant difference ($-0.57\text{ pp}$, $p=0.5782$ under primary single-model evaluation; $+0.15\text{ pp}$, $p=0.9789$ under secondary 15-model probability ensembling), while exposing cross-modality reference label-prior shift from Dreem 2 home EEG (44.2% Light and 18.9% Deep across night-averaged EEG; 46.2% Light and 18.6% Deep across 137,801 pooled sensor-valid epochs; 21.14% Deep of Total Sleep Time [TST] on evaluation nights) to in-lab PSG (57.7% Light and 11.1% Deep of total epochs; 12.01% Deep of TST on $n=25$ quality-clean recordings) that drives systematic Deep sleep ($+10.65\%$) and rapid eye movement (REM; $+10.13\%$) percentage overestimation across both architectures.

### Implications of all the available evidence
Together, these findings define three concrete clinical and deployment guidelines for consumer wearable sleep staging. First, consumer smartwatch IHR and accelerometry reliably track macroscopic sleep duration (TST Pearson $r=0.9492$ for multi-night habitual averages on BIDSleep; $r=0.9091$ for zero-shot single-night PSG) and multi-night habitual Sleep Efficiency (SE; $r=0.8775$, mean absolute error [MAE] 3.03%), whereas single-night external PSG SE shows low absolute error (MAE 3.58%) but reduced rank correlation ($r=0.3781$) due to empirical Wake After Sleep Onset (WASO) underestimation ($-13.57\text{ min}$), and internal stage-architecture proportions (Deep% and REM% of TST) remain vulnerable to cross-modality label-prior shift and cannot yet substitute for clinical PSG. Second, patient-level supervised fine-tuning should not be relied upon after only two calibration nights (inconclusive confirmatory outcome), but should instead be deployed when at least $k \ge 3$ labeled calibration nights are available and paired with pre-adaptation stage-coverage abstention to prevent catastrophic forgetting. Third, prospective multi-site external validation in clinical sleep-disorder cohorts (such as PhysioNet DREAMT v2.2.0) and paediatric or neurodevelopmental multi-night cohorts (such as the Simons Sleep Project, OpenNeuro DS0000089) is essential before clinical diagnostic deployment.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Clinical and Wearable Context
Sleep architecture is scored clinically under American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guidelines [8] into four canonical states: Wakefulness ($W$), Light sleep (combining Non-Rapid Eye Movement [NREM] stages N1 and N2), Deep slow-wave sleep (NREM stage N3), and Rapid Eye Movement (REM) sleep ($R$). While laboratory polysomnography (PSG) remains the diagnostic gold standard, its cost, obtrusive sensor montage, technologist scoring burden, and single-night susceptibility to the first-night effect preclude longitudinal multi-night monitoring in naturalistic home environments [1,2].

Consumer smartwatches equipped with optical photoplethysmography (PPG) and tri-axial accelerometers offer scalable longitudinal tracking by inferring sleep states from peripheral autonomic nervous system (ANS) tone and somatic immobility [1–3]. However, mapping low-rate instantaneous heart rate (IHR; ~0.2 Hz via Apple HealthKit) and wrist movement to central EEG-defined sleep stages is an ill-posed inverse problem subject to pronounced inter-individual biological heterogeneity in resting heart rate, autonomic responsivity, and circadian phase [1,3].

### 1.2 The Promise and Peril of Personalisation
Model personalisation—fine-tuning a population backbone on a few initial home nights paired with a convenient dry-sensor EEG headband—offers a practical route to bridge inter-individual domain shift [3]. Yet unconstrained gradient adaptation on small, class-imbalanced personal calibration sets ($k=1\text{–}4$ nights) risks catastrophic forgetting and negative transfer, wherein fine-tuning degrades subsequent staging accuracy below the unmodified zero-shot population model [3]. Furthermore, deploying recurrent neural architectures on consumer wearables requires balancing classification accuracy against strict memory, latency, and energy budgets on smartwatch system-on-chip (SoC) or companion smartphone hardware.

### 1.3 Addressed Research Gaps and Claim Boundaries
This study addresses three foundational gaps in translational wearable sleep staging. First, regarding personalisation thresholds and safety in healthy adults (Gap G1), although supervised wrist-PPG personalisation was recently demonstrated in sleep-disordered patients [3], it remains unknown how many labeled home nights ($k=1\text{–}4$) are needed for low-rate (~0.2 Hz) consumer smartwatch IHR adaptation in healthy adults under strictly chronological, leakage-free, pre-registered rules, or how to safeguard against severe negative transfer when calibration nights lack specific sleep stages. Second, regarding protocol-matched efficiency trade-offs (Gap G2), published deep sleep-staging benchmarks frequently suffer from subject or night split leakage and 5-hour nocturnal cropping biases [1,5]; eliminating these confounders is necessary to quantify the exact accuracy–efficiency trade-off between a compact edge-quantised sequence model and the 33.34-fold larger published SLAMSS-IFS server architecture [1] under identical 5-fold cross-validation. Third, regarding external single-night clinical PSG generalisation and agreement (Gap G3), we evaluate whether within-cohort performance translates strictly zero-shot to independent gold-standard in-lab PSG [2] and quantify clinical sleep-summary agreement across Total Sleep Time (TST), Sleep Onset Latency (SOL), Wake After Sleep Onset (WASO), Sleep Efficiency (SE), Deep sleep percentage of TST (Deep%), and REM sleep percentage of TST (REM%) via Bland–Altman analysis [4].

We enforce explicit epistemological and clinical claim boundaries throughout this work [7]. We do not claim to present the first multi-night personalisation study [3], nor do we claim universal personalisation efficacy. Our locked primary confirmatory hypothesis for two-night fine-tuning is preserved strictly as inconclusive against its pre-registered $+1.50\text{ pp}$ superiority threshold. Non-significant paired differences between Model A and SLAMSS-IFS on external PSG are framed strictly as showing no statistically significant difference (empirical parity under paired testing) rather than formal statistical equivalence under two one-sided tests (TOST), because no clinical equivalence margin was pre-specified. Finally, all findings are scoped to healthy adults and offline full-night inference buffered on a smartwatch SoC or companion smartphone.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Study Design, Datasets, and Participant Flow
We conducted a multi-stage computational validation across two independent, publicly available human sleep datasets in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis with Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD+AI) statement [7]. Because both datasets consist entirely of de-identified, publicly archived human recordings released under open data-sharing credentials, secondary computational analysis was exempt from additional local institutional review board approval; original data collection received ethical approval from the University of Massachusetts Amherst Institutional Review Board (BIDSleep [1]) and the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (SleepAccel [2]), with written informed consent obtained from all participants.

The internal multi-night development and personalisation cohort was BIDSleep ($N=47$), collected at the University of Massachusetts Amherst [1] and distributed via PhysioNet (DOI: `10.13026/rees-1092`) under the Open Data Commons Attribution License (ODC-By v1.0). BIDSleep comprises 47 healthy adults (participant identifiers `Bidslab00` through `Bidslab68`) wearing an Apple Watch Series 6 (recording Apple HealthKit IHR at a median effective sampling rate of 0.197 Hz, interquartile range [IQR] 0.073 Hz, nightly median heart rate 64.0 beats per min [BPM], alongside tri-axial accelerometry) and a 5-channel dry-sensor Dreem 2 EEG headband scored by a credentialed sleep technologist for up to seven home nights. Of 253 recorded sessions, 7 nights across 6 participants failed a pre-registered $<60\%$ synchronized sensor coverage threshold (`Bidslab08` Night 7 [19.3%], `Bidslab16` Night 2 [24.0%], `Bidslab34` Night 1 [58.7%], `Bidslab42` Nights 1 [30.1%] and 3 [1.1%], `Bidslab49` Night 1 [12.4%], and `Bidslab60` Night 1 [33.8%]), leaving 246 usable nights across 47 participants. Across all labeled Dreem 2 EEG epochs prior to sensor masking, the unweighted night-averaged reference stage distribution was 44.2% Light, 18.9% Deep, 24.1% REM, and 12.8% Wake; across the 137,801 synchronized sensor-valid 30-second epochs used for model training and evaluation (with unscored or artifact epochs masked), the pooled distribution was 11.9% Wake, 46.2% Light, 18.6% Deep, and 23.2% REM (corresponding to 21.14% Deep and 29.67% REM as percentages of TST excluding Wake across the $N=46$ evaluation cohort).

Three nested analytical sub-cohorts were defined a priori within BIDSleep. First, the population baseline cohort ($N=47$, 246 nights) included all 47 participants with at least two usable nights, evaluated via subject-disjoint 5-fold outer cross-validation. Second, the two-night adaptation cohort ($N=46$, 152 evaluation nights) enforced the pre-registered eligibility rule requiring at least $k + 1$ usable nights ($n_{\text{usable}} \ge 3$ for $k=2$ calibration nights); participant `Bidslab49` had only two usable nights (leaving zero post-calibration evaluation nights at $k=2$) and was excluded a priori. Third, for the calibration-depth learning curve across $k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ chronological calibration nights, we defined $E_k$ as each participant's post-calibration evaluation nights $k+1 \dots n_i$. To maintain a consistent participant base with the adaptation cohort, available-cohort sensitivity analysis ($E_k$) at $k=1$ was restricted to the same $N=46$ adaptation cohort ($n_{\text{usable}} \ge 3$; 246 total nights minus 2 nights from `Bidslab49` minus 46 first calibration nights $= 198$ evaluation nights), followed by $N=46$ at $k=2$ (152 nights), $N=42$ at $k=3$ (106 nights), and $N=33$ at $k=4$ (64 nights). To isolate calibration-depth scaling from changing participant denominators, our primary calibration-depth analysis evaluated the fixed common cohort of $N=33$ participants on their identical post-night-4 evaluation set ($E_4$, 64 nights).

The external zero-shot validation cohort was PhysioNet SleepAccel v1.0.0 ($N=31$) [2] (DOI: `10.13026/hmhs-py35`), an independent single-night Apple Watch and in-lab PSG dataset collected at the University of Michigan comprising 31 healthy adults, each contributing one single-night laboratory PSG recording. Under our locked external validation protocol, pre-flight quality screening excluded 6 participants prior to comparative inference: 5 incomplete or short PSG recordings with fewer than 600 thirty-second epochs (`3509524` [417 epochs], `4018081` [510 epochs], `46343` [567 epochs], `5132496` [465 epochs], and `759667` [475 epochs]) and 1 recording with severe Apple Watch accelerometer motion dropout (`7749105` [960 epochs; valid accelerometer motion coverage 13.65%]). This yielded $n=25$ quality-clean primary participants and $N=31$ all-subject sensitivity participants. Across SleepAccel, in-lab PSG exhibited a reference stage prior of 57.7% Light and 11.1% Deep across total recording epochs (12.01% Deep and 25.21% REM as percentages of TST on the $n=25$ quality-clean cohort). Exactly three SleepAccel participants had zero reference PSG epochs for a specific sleep stage: `1455390` (0 Deep sleep epochs; included in the $n=25$ quality-clean primary cohort) and `5132496` and `7749105` (0 REM sleep epochs; both belonging to the 6 quality-excluded recordings and therefore affecting only the $N=31$ sensitivity cohort). Zero-support stage F1 scores were defined deterministically as 0.0 (zero undefined values).

### 2.2 Input Representation, Architectures, and Edge Quantisation
Full-night nocturnal sequences of length $L=1200$ thirty-second epochs (10.0 hours; zero-padded if shorter, or stitched via 50%-overlapping sliding-window logits if longer) were constructed from 1 Hz resampled IHR, tri-axial accelerometry magnitude, jerk, and energy features, and robust per-night median and IQR normalisation. Full-night context ($L=1200$) was selected over the 5-hour cropping protocol ($L=600$) used in prior benchmarks [1] because 5-hour cropping discards 28% of valid nocturnal epochs, disproportionately depletes late-night REM sleep, and reduced Model A cross-validated Macro-F1 from 64.79% to 63.06%. To prevent circadian time-of-night shortcut learning, both architectures received identical circadian-neutralised subject metadata (retaining effective sampling frequency and nightly heart-rate mean and standard deviation while zeroing clock-time cosine and sine encodings).

Frozen Model A is a compact 1D convolutional-recurrent network comprising a three-stage 1D convolutional epoch encoder (kernel sizes $\{3, 5, 5\}$, channel widths $\{32, 64, 128\}$, spatial dropout $p=0.20$) followed by a 2-layer Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (BiGRU; hidden dimension $h=64$ per direction, 128-dimensional bidirectional state) and a linear classification head, totaling 163,940 trainable parameters. We compared Model A against the official SLAMSS-IFS baseline [1] implemented from the authors' public repository. Whereas the published paper text [1] describes a 666,060-parameter network, inspection of the official repository revealed an additional 4.70-million-parameter multi-scale convolutional fusion block (`cnn3`), bringing the actual official architecture to 5,465,768 trainable parameters (an 8.2-fold repository-versus-paper discrepancy). We trained official SLAMSS-IFS under identical 5-fold subject-disjoint splits using zero teacher-forcing (eliminating upstream ground-truth label leakage at step $t=0$) and the authors' 32-bit floating-point (FP32) composite cross-entropy plus 0.01-weighted asymmetric square-root inverse-frequency cost-matrix loss (regularised class-sensitive cost loss; micro-batch size 4, 40–100 epochs, early-stopping patience 25). Model A achieves a 33.34-fold parameter reduction versus the official SLAMSS-IFS code and a 4.06-fold reduction versus the paper text.

To quantify edge deployability, we applied post-training dynamic INT8 quantisation in PyTorch, which quantises all recurrent BiGRU and linear classification layers (112,932 of 163,940 parameters; 68.89%) to 8-bit integers while retaining 1D convolutional and normalisation layers in FP32 (static post-training quantisation [PTQ] is unsupported in eager/FX graph mode without refactoring residual additions and packed padded sequences). We profiled exact multiply–accumulate operations (MMACs), in-memory and serialised weight footprints, and full-night ($L=1200$) CPU inference latency across 4-thread (4T) and 1-thread (1T) execution. Because the 2-layer BiGRU processes the full-night sequence bidirectionally, edge execution requires offline full-night feature buffering on a smartwatch SoC or companion smartphone rather than 1-epoch causal streaming on a 512 KB static random-access memory (SRAM) microcontroller.

### 2.3 Personalisation Protocols, Controls, and Exploratory Closures
Under our pre-registered within-person adaptation protocol, five experimental arms were evaluated per held-out participant on chronological evaluation nights ($k \ge 3$). The zero-shot baseline (`ZS`) evaluated frozen population Model A without parameter updates. Confirmatory two-night supervised fine-tuning (`FT-2`) adapted Model A on each participant's first $k=2$ chronological nights using cross-entropy loss, the Adam optimiser, batch size $B=2$, gradient norm clipping at 1.0, and nested inner-validation selection of learning rate, epoch count, and layer-unfreezing depth (selecting between adapting only the linear classification head, the BiGRU plus linear head, or the full network). Two supervised control arms isolated the mechanism of adaptation: donor fine-tuning (`FT-other-2`, Specificity Hypothesis `S1`) applied identical two-night fine-tuning using two nights from an unrelated donor participant, and from-scratch personal training (`PS-2`, Prior Hypothesis `S2`) trained the same architecture from random weight initialisation on the target participant's two calibration nights.

For unsupervised two-night adaptation (`UA-2`, Unsupervised Hypothesis `S3`), an initial pre-registered execution returned a 0.00 pp no-op because an input-scaling unit mismatch triggered a pre-flight physiological heart-rate abort gate. Following a documented pre-registration software quality amendment, we remediated the unit scaling and re-executed quality-gated unsupervised InfoMax adaptation—minimising conditional epoch-level prediction entropy while maximising batch-level marginal class entropy across the BiGRU and linear classification head—over 81,233 calibration epochs (97.26% valid wearable coverage, zero non-physiological leakage) across all 46 participants.

To characterise multi-night scaling, we evaluated supervised fine-tuning across $k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ chronological calibration nights on both the fixed common cohort ($E_4$, $N=33$) and the available cohorts ($E_k$, $N=46, 46, 42, 33$). During post-execution audit of the multi-depth driver script, an in-place weight-mutation defect was identified wherein un-reset zero-shot evaluations on subsequent fold participants inherited prior-participant fine-tuned weights (yielding a contaminated 62.86% baseline matching donor fine-tuning); this was resolved via CPU-only clean frozen-checkpoint zero-shot reconciliation (verifying clean zero-shot baselines of 63.54% on $E_4$ [$N=33$] and 64.58% on $E_2$ [$N=46$]), while all fine-tuned arms were verified to have reset cleanly from the frozen population backbone.

Finally, we conducted two design-informed exploratory analyses formulated after inspecting the primary personalisation and 5-fold baseline results (explicitly disclosed as post-hoc exploratory closures for which confirmatory labels are prohibited). First, an exploratory heterogeneity audit tested six pre-specified baseline physiological, signal-quality, and stage-distribution covariates (minimum calibration-night stage fraction, calibration stage Shannon entropy, effective IHR sampling frequency, sensor missingness rate, baseline heart-rate mean, and baseline heart-rate IQR) against participant-level adaptation gain (`FT-2` minus `ZS`, $N=46$) and architecture gap (Model A minus SLAMSS-IFS, $N=47$) using Spearman rank correlation with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) control and Oldham's headroom check [10] (correlating change $Y - X$ against the average $(X + Y)/2$ to avoid mathematical coupling). Second, an exploratory safe-adaptation study ($N=46$) evaluated inner-validation-selected Kullback–Leibler divergence regularisation toward the frozen zero-shot logits (`FT-2-KL`, $\lambda_{\text{KL}} \in \{0.1, 1.0, 10.0\}$), pre-adaptation stage-coverage gating (`GATE_tau`, abstaining and reverting to frozen `ZS` whenever a participant's minimum calibration-night stage proportion $\min_c P_{\text{cal}}(Y=c)$ fell below threshold $\tau = 0.01$), and their combination (`FT-2-KL_GATE_tau`), tracking the number of harmed participants experiencing severe negative transfer $\le -5.0\text{ pp}$ ($N_{\text{harm}}$).

### 2.4 Statistical Analysis and Clinical Agreement
The primary classification metric was unweighted 4-class Macro-F1, computed at the participant level after averaging across three random training seeds (42, 123, and 2024). Across the 5 outer folds and 3 random seeds, training produced $5 \times 3 = 15$ frozen population checkpoints for both Model A and SLAMSS-IFS. Paired comparisons used 10,000-resample participant-level percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CIs; random seed 42) and two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (using exact permutation distributions where specified), alongside paired effect sizes (Cohen's $d_z$) and Holm–Bonferroni step-down multiple-testing corrections. Under the locked confirmatory decision rule for two-night fine-tuning (`H-A`), superiority required the 95% bootstrap CI lower bound to exceed 0.00 pp and the point estimate or upper bound to support a clinically meaningful $+1.50\text{ pp}$ gain; intervals spanning both zero and $+1.50\text{ pp}$ were classified a priori as inconclusive. Monotonicity across $k=1\text{–}4$ calibration nights on $E_4$ ($N=33$) was tested via Page's $L$ trend test for ordered alternatives [9].

For external zero-shot validation on PhysioNet SleepAccel, we evaluated all 15 frozen Model A checkpoints against all 15 frozen SLAMSS-IFS checkpoints under two pre-specified inference modes: primary single-model mean evaluation (computing classification metrics and sleep summaries independently for each of the 15 checkpoints and averaging the 15 scores per participant, reflecting realistic single-checkpoint edge deployment) and secondary 15-model probability ensemble evaluation (averaging the 15 epoch-level softmax probability distributions prior to $\arg\max$ stage assignment). Clinical sleep-summary agreement across TST, SOL, WASO, SE, Deep%, and REM% was quantified via Bland–Altman analysis [4], reporting mean bias ($\bar{d}$) with $t$-distribution 95% CIs, standard deviation of paired differences ($s_d$), 95% parametric limits of agreement ($\text{LoA} = \bar{d} \pm 1.96 s_d$), exact standard errors and 95% CIs on the LoA bounds ($\text{SE}(\text{LoA}) = s_d \sqrt{3/N}$), MAE, Pearson correlation ($r$), ordinary least-squares proportional-bias slope of difference on average, and Shapiro–Wilk normality testing of paired differences ($p < 0.05$ triggering non-parametric median bias and 2.5th–97.5th percentile LoA fallbacks).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Matched 5-Fold Cross-Validation and Edge Deployment Profiling ($N=47$)
Under identical 5-fold subject-disjoint evaluation across BIDSleep ($N=47$, 246 nights, 3 seeds; **Table 1**), frozen Model A (163,940 parameters) achieved $64.79\% \pm 7.45$ Macro-F1 ($66.18\% \pm 6.79$ overall accuracy, Cohen's $\kappa = 0.5078 \pm 0.1011$), retaining 96.2% of the Macro-F1 performance of the 33.34-fold larger official SLAMSS-IFS architecture (5,465,768 parameters; $67.37\% \pm 6.73$ Macro-F1, $68.75\% \pm 6.08$ accuracy, $\kappa = 0.5378 \pm 0.0941$). The paired Macro-F1 difference was $-2.58\text{ pp}$ (95% bootstrap CI $-3.53\text{ to }-1.68$, Wilcoxon $p=1.9818\times 10^{-6}$, Cohen's $d_z=-0.780$, with Model A outperforming SLAMSS-IFS in 13 of 47 participants). On REM F1 ($-2.41\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $-4.68\text{ to }-0.32$), paired rank testing was not statistically significant ($p=0.1047$, Model A winning 19 of 47 participants) amid high inter-individual variance, indicating no statistically significant rank-order difference rather than formal equivalence.

Edge hardware profiling confirmed that Model A requires 794.05 MMACs (1,588,108,800 floating-point operations, bitwise exact) per 10-hour nocturnal sequence ($L=1200$), executing in $47.73 \pm 0.49\text{ ms}$ on a 4-thread CPU and $69.39 \pm 1.48\text{ ms}$ on a single CPU thread. Dynamic INT8 quantisation reduced the in-memory weight footprint to 309.54 KiB (339.99 KiB serialised file; theoretical all-INT8 weight floor 160.10 KiB) and incurred zero classification accuracy loss across all 47 participants ($+0.0011\text{ pp}$ Macro-F1 vs FP32 Model A, 95% CI $-0.0277\text{ to }+0.0312$, Wilcoxon $p=0.7892$, $d_z=+0.010$), whereas official SLAMSS-IFS required 21.86 MB of FP32 weights and 5.12 GiB of graphics processing unit (GPU) video memory during training at batch size 4.

### 3.2 Confirmatory Two-Night Personalisation ($N=46$) and Controls (`S1`–`S3`)
Across $N=46$ eligible participants (152 evaluation nights), two-night supervised fine-tuning (`FT-2`) increased Macro-F1 from 64.58% (`ZS`) to 65.59%, yielding a mean paired gain of $+1.01\text{ pp}$ ($+1.0054\text{ pp}$, 95% bootstrap CI $-0.17\text{ to }+1.99\text{ pp}$ [exact $-0.1724\text{ to }+1.9928\text{ pp}$], Wilcoxon $W=246.0$, $p=0.00131$, with 34 of 46 participants [73.9%] improving). Because the 95% bootstrap CI lower bound spanned zero while the upper bound exceeded the pre-registered $+1.50\text{ pp}$ clinical superiority threshold, the primary confirmatory hypothesis (`H-A`) evaluated strictly to inconclusive.

Pre-registered specificity, prior, and unsupervised controls confirmed that the observed adaptation gains reflect person-specific supervised representation refinement rather than generic fine-tuning artifacts. For Specificity Hypothesis `S1` (`FT-2` vs donor fine-tuning `FT-other-2`), personal fine-tuning outperformed donor fine-tuning by $+2.73 \pm 5.78\text{ pp}$ (10,000-resample percentile bootstrap 95% CI $+1.05\text{ to }+4.32\text{ pp}$; parametric $t$-based 95% CI $+1.01\text{ to }+4.44\text{ pp}$, $t=3.204$, $p=0.00249$; two-sided Wilcoxon $W=169.0$, $p=1.89\times 10^{-5}$, one-sided $p=9.0\times 10^{-6}$), partly reflecting negative transfer from mismatched donor nights (`FT-other-2` fell 1.72 pp below `ZS` to 62.86%). Excluding two opposing extreme outliers (`Bidslab68` $+19.98\text{ pp}$ and `Bidslab34` $-20.79\text{ pp}$; $N=44$) preserved strong specificity ($+2.87 \pm 3.89\text{ pp}$, $p=1.40\times 10^{-5}$). For Prior Hypothesis `S2` (`FT-2` vs from-scratch personal training `PS-2`), personal fine-tuning outperformed de novo two-night training (45.27% Macro-F1) by $+20.32 \pm 9.14\text{ pp}$ (10,000-resample percentile bootstrap 95% CI $+17.70\text{ to }+22.94\text{ pp}$; parametric $t$-based 95% CI $+17.60\text{ to }+23.03\text{ pp}$, $t=15.07$, $p=3.27\times 10^{-19}$; two-sided Wilcoxon $p=3.52\times 10^{-9}$, one-sided $p=1.76\times 10^{-9}$), confirming that two calibration nights cannot support representation learning without population pre-training. For Unsupervised Hypothesis `S3` (remediated quality-gated InfoMax adaptation `UA-2` vs `ZS`), adapting the BiGRU and classification head without EEG labels across all 46 participants (138 runs, 0% class-collapse rate, mean final marginal entropy 1.382 nats) increased Macro-F1 from 64.58% to 64.79% ($+0.21\text{ pp}$, 95% bootstrap CI $+0.04\text{ to }+0.39\text{ pp}$, one-sided superiority $p=0.0322$, two-sided $p=0.0645$, 27 of 46 [58.7%] wins). Under pre-registered decision rules, this represents a statistically significant sub-benchmark gain (below the $+1.00\text{ pp}$ practical benchmark), indicating that label-free entropy adaptation acts only as a mild auxiliary regulariser and cannot replace labeled calibration.

### 3.4 Calibration-Depth Learning Curve ($k=1\text{–}4$ Nights)
On the fixed common cohort of $N=33$ participants evaluated across their identical post-night-4 evaluation set ($E_4$, 64 nights; clean reconciled zero-shot baseline $\text{ZS} = 63.54\%$ Macro-F1), within-person fine-tuning improved monotonically with calibration depth, with 24 of 33 participants (72.7%) improving over zero-shot at every calibration depth $k$. Specifically, Macro-F1 reached 65.28% at $k=1$ ($+1.75\text{ pp}$, 95% bootstrap CI $+0.63\text{ to }+2.93$, exact two-sided Wilcoxon $p=0.00879$, all-$k$ Holm-adjusted $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.02637$), 65.41% at $k=2$ ($+1.88\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $+0.59\text{ to }+3.25$, $p=0.00402$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.01609$), 65.82% at $k=3$ ($+2.29\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $+0.69\text{ to }+3.97$, $p=0.01369$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.02637$), and 66.25% at $k=4$ ($+2.71\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $+1.17\text{ to }+4.33$, $p=0.00170$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.00848$). Page's $L$ trend test [9] confirmed a statistically significant monotonic upward trajectory across $k=1\text{–}4$ ($L=863.0$, expected $\mu_L=825.0$, $\sigma_L=16.58$, $z=2.261$, $p=0.01187$). Under the strict pre-registered 5-test family comprising Page's $L$, $k=3$ versus clean zero-shot, and the three adjacent incremental step deltas ($\delta_{2-1} = +0.13\text{ pp}$, $\delta_{3-2} = +0.41\text{ pp}$, $\delta_{4-3} = +0.42\text{ pp}$), directional one-sided Holm–Bonferroni testing passed $\alpha=0.05$ ($k=3$ $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.03423$; Page's $L$ and $\delta_{4-3}$ $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.04748$), whereas exact two-sided step-down sensitivity narrowly missed the family-wise threshold ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.05935$). Because these $E_4$ adaptation scores ($N=33$, 64 later chronological nights, clean $\text{ZS} = 63.54\%$) are evaluated on a restricted post-calibration sub-cohort, their absolute Macro-F1 percentages cannot be directly compared against full-cohort zero-shot SLAMSS-IFS ($N=47$, 246 nights, 67.37% vs Model A 64.79%).

In the full available-cohort sensitivity analysis ($E_k$, evaluating all post-calibration nights $k+1 \dots n_i$), single-night calibration at $k=1$ ($N=46$, 198 nights, clean $\text{ZS} = 63.95\%$) yielded a non-significant $+0.23\text{ pp}$ change (64.18%, 95% CI $-0.59\text{ to }+1.05$, $p=0.5957$, 25 of 46 wins), and two-night calibration at $k=2$ ($N=46$, 152 nights, clean $\text{ZS} = 64.58\%$) yielded $+0.99\text{ pp}$ (65.58%, 95% CI $-0.18\text{ to }+1.99$, $p=0.00271$, 33 of 46 wins; harmonising within 0.01 pp with the locked confirmatory `FT-2` result of $+1.01\text{ pp}$ [34 of 46 wins], with the minor difference arising solely from micro-batch order in the multi-depth driver). Crucially, three-night calibration at $k=3$ ($N=42$, 106 nights, clean $\text{ZS} = 64.25\%$) was the first calibration depth in the available cohort whose 95% bootstrap CI excluded zero ($+1.42\text{ pp}$ to 65.67%, 95% CI $+0.15\text{ to }+2.58$, $p=0.00232$, 29 of 42 wins), rising further to $+2.71\text{ pp}$ at $k=4$ ($N=33$, 64 nights). This establishes an empirical $k \ge 3$ calibration-night threshold on low-rate smartwatch IHR that converges with clinical PPG personalisation findings in sleep-disordered patients [3].

### 3.5 Exploratory Heterogeneity and Safe Adaptation
*(Prior-Access Exploratory Disclosure: The covariate heterogeneity audit and stage-coverage gating analyses were designed after inspecting the primary personalisation and 5-fold cross-validation results; they are strictly hypothesis-generating exploratory findings and do not alter the inconclusive confirmatory verdict for two-night fine-tuning.)*

In the exploratory heterogeneity audit across 12 primary Spearman rank correlation tests (evaluating 6 baseline physiological, sampling-quality, and stage-distribution predictors against two-night personalisation gain [$N=46$] and Model A-versus-SLAMSS-IFS architecture difference [$N=47$]), zero of 12 predictors reached Benjamini–Hochberg FDR significance ($q < 0.05$; all within-family $q_{\text{family}} \ge 0.5673$, pooled $q_{\text{pooled}} \ge 0.5913$). Oldham's headroom check [10] confirmed the absence of baseline performance coupling ($\rho = +0.0376$, $p=0.8043$), demonstrating that routine baseline summary covariates do not linearly predict average adaptation gain.

Instead, diagnostic inspection revealed that extreme negative transfer under two-night fine-tuning was driven by discrete calibration-night class starvation—most notably in participant `Bidslab34` ($-16.34\text{ pp}$ Macro-F1 collapse), whose two calibration nights contained only 0.68% (15 epochs) of Deep slow-wave sleep. In the exploratory safe-adaptation comparison across $N=46$ participants, Kullback–Leibler divergence regularisation (`FT-2-KL`) achieved 65.62% Macro-F1 ($+1.03\text{ pp}$ vs `ZS`, 95% CI $-0.11\text{ to }+2.01$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0030$, $N_{\text{harm}}=3$ participants with $\le -5.0\text{ pp}$ harm) but failed to prevent catastrophic outlier collapse (worst participant $-15.14\text{ pp}$). Conversely, pre-adaptation stage-coverage gating (`GATE_tau`, abstaining to frozen `ZS` whenever any sleep stage represented less than $\tau = 0.01$ [1.0%] of calibration epochs) achieved 65.91% Macro-F1 ($+1.33\text{ pp}$ vs `ZS`, 95% CI $+0.49\text{ to }+2.12$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0028$, $N_{\text{harm}}=2$). Stage-coverage gating surgically abstained on 2 of 46 participants—including `Bidslab34` ($0.0068 < 0.01$)—reducing worst-case participant collapse by 61% from $-16.34\text{ pp}$ (`FT-2`) to $-6.40\text{ pp}$, whereas combining KL regularisation with gating (`FT-2-KL_GATE_tau`: 65.59%, $+1.00\text{ pp}$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0030$) offered no additional benefit.

### 3.6 Zero-Shot External Single-Night PSG Validation ($N=31$; $n=25$ Quality-Clean Primary)
When all 15 frozen Model A checkpoints (5 outer folds $\times$ 3 random seeds) and all 15 frozen official SLAMSS-IFS checkpoints were evaluated strictly zero-shot on independent PhysioNet SleepAccel single-night in-lab PSG recordings (**Table 3**), SLAMSS-IFS's 2.58 pp BIDSleep Macro-F1 advantage attenuated by 78% to a non-significant $-0.57\text{ pp}$ under primary single-model mean evaluation on the quality-clean cohort ($n=25$: Model A 60.51% vs SLAMSS-IFS 61.08%, 95% bootstrap CI $-2.13\text{ to }+0.94\text{ pp}$, Wilcoxon $p=0.5782$, Cohen's $d_z=-0.14$, 10 wins / 15 losses / 0 ties). Under secondary 15-model probability ensemble evaluation on the quality-clean cohort ($n=25$), Model A achieved 62.94% versus 62.79% for SLAMSS-IFS ($+0.15\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $-1.51\text{ to }+1.86\text{ pp}$, $p=0.9789$, $d_z=+0.03$, 10 wins / 15 losses / 0 ties). In the all-subject sensitivity cohort ($N=31$), paired Macro-F1 differences similarly showed no statistically significant difference under both primary single-model evaluation ($-1.62\text{ pp}$, 60.65% vs 62.27%, 95% CI $-3.84\text{ to }+0.28\text{ pp}$, $p=0.3468$, $d_z=-0.27$) and secondary probability ensembling ($-1.20\text{ pp}$, 62.94% vs 64.14%, 95% CI $-3.96\text{ to }+1.04\text{ pp}$, $p=0.5551$, $d_z=-0.17$). Across stage-specific F1 scores on the quality-clean cohort ($n=25$), Light sleep was the sole stage where SLAMSS-IFS retained a statistically significant advantage ($-3.49\text{ pp}$, $p=0.0006$ primary; $-2.56\text{ pp}$, $p=0.0088$ secondary), whereas Model A showed numerically higher point estimates on Wake F1 ($+1.95\text{ pp}$, $p=0.1336$ primary; $+2.65\text{ pp}$, $p=0.1209$ secondary), primary REM F1 ($+0.60\text{ pp}$, $p=0.4418$), and secondary Deep F1 ($+1.13\text{ pp}$, $p=0.8192$).

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Translational Synthesis: Efficiency, External Generalisation, and Label-Prior Shift
Our matched 5-fold cross-validation and strictly zero-shot external PSG evaluations reveal a central translational lesson for digital sleep medicine: in-distribution server architecture complexity does not guarantee superior external clinical PSG generalisation. Although the 5.47-million-parameter official SLAMSS-IFS model outperformed the 163,940-parameter Model A by $+2.58\text{ pp}$ Macro-F1 on BIDSleep ($N=47$), 78% of that advantage vanished on independent PhysioNet SleepAccel single-night in-lab PSG ($-0.57\text{ pp}$, $p=0.5782$ under primary single-model evaluation; $+0.15\text{ pp}$, $p=0.9789$ under secondary 15-model probability ensembling), with Model A achieving lower absolute error on external TST, WASO, and SE, fewer proportional-bias distortions, and higher REM% correlation. Dynamic INT8 quantisation (309.54 KiB in-memory weights, 47.7 ms per night on CPU with $+0.0011\text{ pp}$ Macro-F1 retention) makes Model A immediately deployable on smartwatch SoCs or companion smartphones via offline full-night buffering.

Equally important for clinical translation is the cross-modality reference label-prior shift uncovered in our external Bland–Altman audit. Both architectures—trained on BIDSleep Dreem 2 dry-sensor home EEG (44.2% Light and 18.9% Deep across night-averaged EEG; 46.2% Light and 18.6% Deep across 137,801 pooled sensor-valid epochs; 21.14% Deep and 29.67% REM of TST on evaluation nights)—systematically overestimated Deep sleep ($+10.65\%$ primary / $+10.23\%$ secondary for Model A; $+8.67\%$ / $+8.84\%$ for SLAMSS-IFS) and REM sleep ($+10.13\%$ / $+9.35\%$ for Model A; $+7.21\%$ / $+6.87\%$ for SLAMSS-IFS) when evaluated zero-shot against SleepAccel single-night in-lab PSG (57.7% Light and 11.1% Deep of total epochs; 12.01% Deep and 25.21% REM of TST on $n=25$ quality-clean recordings). Furthermore, while consumer wearables reliably track macroscopic sleep duration across both multi-night habitual averages ($r=0.9492$) and single-night external PSG ($r=0.9091$), and track multi-night habitual Sleep Efficiency strongly ($r=0.8775$, MAE 3.03%), single-night external PSG Sleep Efficiency exhibits low absolute error (MAE 3.58%) but modest rank correlation ($r=0.3781$) due to empirical single-night WASO underestimation ($-13.57\text{ min}$). Consequently, macroscopic sleep duration and multi-night habitual efficiency are ready for longitudinal tracking, whereas stage-architecture percentages require modality-specific prior calibration before clinical interpretation.

### 4.2 Actionable Personalisation Thresholds, Safety Gating, and Statistical Power
Within-person adaptation with ~0.2 Hz Apple HealthKit IHR is genuinely person-specific (outperforming donor-night fine-tuning by $+2.73\text{ pp}$) and scales monotonically with calibration depth (Page's $L$ $p=0.01187$ [9]), converging with clinical raw-PPG findings [3] at a threshold of at least $k \ge 3$ calibration nights ($+1.42\text{ pp}$ in the available cohort where the 95% CI first excludes zero; $+2.29\text{ pp}$ in the common cohort). However, unconstrained fine-tuning on only $k=2$ nights remains statistically inconclusive at $N=46$ ($+1.01\text{ pp}$ against the locked $+1.50\text{ pp}$ threshold; given the observed paired standard deviation of ~3.7 pp, detecting a true $+1.0\text{ pp}$ gain at 80% statistical power with $\alpha=0.05$ requires approximately $N \approx 107$ multi-night participants) and risks catastrophic negative transfer when a calibration recording lacks adequate stage coverage (`Bidslab34`: 0.68% Deep sleep, $-16.34\text{ pp}$ collapse). Exploratory stage-coverage gating ($\tau = 0.01$) provides a practical, zero-cost surgical safeguard ($+1.33\text{ pp}$, abstaining on 2 of 46 participants and reducing worst-case collapse by 61% to $-6.40\text{ pp}$).

### 4.3 Limitations and Future Prospective Targets
Our conclusions are bounded by four methodological and clinical limitations. First, our study is strictly scoped to healthy adults; neither BIDSleep nor SleepAccel includes patients with obstructive sleep apnoea, insomnia, or parasomnias, where fragmented sleep and autonomic dysfunction alter heart-rate variability dynamics [3]. Prospective validation in clinical sleep-disorder cohorts such as PhysioNet DREAMT v2.2.0 is required before diagnostic deployment. Second, PhysioNet SleepAccel provides only one in-lab PSG night per participant, precluding external multi-night personalisation testing against gold-standard PSG, while BIDSleep relies on technologist-reviewed Dreem 2 dry-sensor EEG headband labels rather than full laboratory PSG. Prospective multi-night external cohorts such as the Simons Sleep Project (OpenNeuro DS0000089) represent priority targets for independent replication of $k \ge 3$ scaling and stage-coverage gating. Third, because Model A relies on a 2-layer bidirectional GRU over full-night sequences ($L=1200$), inference requires offline full-night buffering on a smartwatch SoC or companion smartphone and cannot support causal real-time closed-loop acoustic or electrical stimulation. Fourth, our covariate heterogeneity audit and stage-coverage gating rule ($\tau = 0.01$) were formulated as design-informed exploratory analyses after inspecting the two-night fine-tuning results and therefore require prospective confirmatory replication in an independent multi-night cohort.
